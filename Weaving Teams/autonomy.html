Autonomy & Self-Organization

Living systems maintain themselves by re-producing their own organization — while being entirely shaped by their environment.

From the biology of autopoiesis (Maturana & Varela) and Morin's auto-eco-organization.

The idea

A cell is the cleanest example of a living system. It produces its own membrane, its own internal structure, its own processes of repair. In that sense it is autonomous — nothing outside it is writing its instructions line by line. And yet a cell is utterly dependent on its environment for matter, energy, and signal. It lives in a constant exchange. Morin's term for this double condition is auto-eco-organization: self-producing and context-coupled, at the same time.

A team is structurally similar. Its real organization — who talks to whom, who decides what, what counts as good work, what gets ignored — is produced from the inside, out of thousands of small interactions. You can try to dictate it from above, but the moment you look away, the team returns to the organization it is actually generating for itself. The leader who doesn't know this exhausts themselves rewriting rules the system keeps re-rewriting.

The alternative is not abdication. It is learning to work with the team's self-organizing character rather than against it. That means shaping what enters and leaves — information, constraints, resources, membership — and then letting the internal structure emerge in response.

What breaks when we ignore it

Micromanagement. Leaders who spend their week redoing other people's work. Roles that look clear on an org chart and make no sense in the actual flow of work. Reorganizations that reshuffle boxes and change nothing. Engagement surveys that keep surfacing the same frustrations year after year. All of these are symptoms of trying to specify internal organization from outside.

In practice

Define constraints, not tasks

Tell the team what must be true (quality, budget, timeline, values), not how to do the work. Constraints create the space for self-organization; tasks collapse it.

Make information symmetric

A self-organizing team requires shared information. Asymmetry forces dependency on whoever holds the information. Start by asking what the team doesn't know that you do.

Move authority to knowledge

The person closest to a decision usually has the best information. If they can't decide, ask why, and fix the reason.

Design the edges

Who joins the team, what enters the team's attention, what leaves the team as output — these are the real levers. The internal arrangement takes care of itself if the edges are well-designed.

A question to sit with

What would happen if I disappeared for a month? What would that tell me about this team's autonomy — and about my role in it?
