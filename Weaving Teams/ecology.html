Ecology of Action

Every action, once launched into a system, is reshaped by the system it enters. Intentions do not control outcomes.

Morin's écologie de l'action: action is a proposal to a system that will respond in its own terms.

The idea

You announce a new performance framework to improve quality. Three months later, quality metrics are up — and engineers are gaming them. You launch a transparency initiative to build trust. Within weeks, the new dashboards have created a subtle chill: people are careful in new ways.

Neither of these is a failure of planning. They are how complex systems respond to inputs. An action launched into a living system is not executed; it is interpreted, rerouted, amplified in unexpected places, dampened in others. The context does not stay still while the action plays out. Morin calls this the ecology of action: once you act, the system you acted on reshapes what you did.

The implication is not to stop acting. It is to drop the illusion that intent equals outcome. Leadership in complex systems is less about executing a plan and more about tending an experiment: launching, sensing, learning, adjusting. The discipline is not rigor about plans; it is rigor about learning.

Three signatures of the ecology of action

Gaming. Any metric that becomes a target loses its meaning (Goodhart's law). The system reorganizes to produce the metric while bypassing the goal. This is not bad behavior; it is the system doing what systems do.

Second-order effects. An action succeeds at its direct aim and produces a downstream effect that dominates the first-order gain. Efficiency drives produce burnout. Transparency produces self-censorship. Empowerment, clumsily done, produces anxiety.

Drift. Outcomes separate from intentions over time. A value that was launched alive becomes procedure; a procedure that was launched alive becomes theater.

In practice

Launch experiments, not plans

Frame a change as a hypothesis you're testing, with a time-box and a sensing mechanism. The language shift changes how the system receives the action.

Distinguish reversible from irreversible decisions

Reversible decisions can be made fast and cheap. Irreversible ones deserve slowness and care. Most organizations confuse the two.

Shorten feedback loops; lengthen learning loops

Fast feedback on whether something is working; slow, honest learning about what the system is teaching you.

Assume second-order effects — name them before acting

The discipline of asking "and then what?" three times is often the difference between mature and naive intervention.

A question to sit with

What unintended consequence is currently teaching me something I haven't yet admitted?
