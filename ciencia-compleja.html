01 · Edgar Morin

The tetralogical loop

Order · Disorder · Interactions · Organization

Morin argues that no phenomenon in the universe can be explained by just one of these four notions. A star is born from the thermal disorder of gas; it gains order through gravity; it sustains itself via interactions between forces; and that dance produces an organization — the star itself — which in turn fuels more interactions.

Each node needs the other three. None comes first. There's no hierarchy: there's a loop.

02 · Cybernetics

Feedback loops

The Lotka-Volterra loop: foxes and rabbits

Wiener's cybernetics showed that a system can regulate itself with no central command, just through loops: when rabbits are abundant, foxes eat well and multiply; when foxes are abundant, rabbits grow scarce; the foxes then starve and decline; the rabbits recover. And the cycle begins again.

There's no static equilibrium. There's oscillation — order in time, not order in a single instant.

Try this Raise the hunting rate (β). What happens if foxes become too efficient? Now lower it a lot: does the ecosystem get boring?
03 · Cellular automata

The Game of Life

Three rules. Whole universes.

Conway devised this game in 1970 with absurdly simple rules: a live cell with 2 or 3 neighbors survives; a dead cell with exactly 3 neighbors is born; everything else dies. And yet, on that grid, gliders appear that travel, oscillators that pulse, and structures that reproduce themselves.

This is the canonical example of emergence: properties of the system that aren't in its parts. The glider doesn't live in any single cell — it lives in the pattern.

Try this Load the Glider and hit play. Now try the Pulsar. Click on the board to draw your own cells. Can you create something that doesn't die?
04 · Stuart Kauffman

NK Boolean networks

The edge of chaos

Kauffman imagined a network of N genes where each one "listens" to K neighbors and decides to turn on or off using a random lookup table. If K=1, the network is boring (everything freezes fast). If K≥3, it's ungovernable chaos. But with K=2 something magical happens: the network settles into stable cycles, as if it "remembered" patterns. Kauffman suggested this is why life is possible — natural selection needed that regime, neither frozen nor chaotic.

There are roughly √N attractors. With N=20 you'll see ~4-5 states the network always returns to.

Try this Set K=1 (frozen), then K=2 (life), then K=4 (chaos). Watch the pattern below: with K=2 there's rhythmic order. At K=4 it looks like TV static.

05 · Prigogine & time

The Lorenz attractor

Determinism + sensitivity = chaos

In 1963 Edward Lorenz was modeling the weather with three simple equations. When he restarted a run with a number rounded to three decimals, he got a completely different result. He had just discovered sensitivity to initial conditions: the famous butterfly effect.

But notice: the trajectory doesn't fly off to infinity. It stays trapped in a wing-shaped figure. It's unpredictable in detail and orderly in aggregate. That's a strange attractor — the heart of complexity.

Try this Turn on "butterfly": two trajectories start almost identical. Watch how, despite obeying the same law, they separate. That's the weather. That's almost everything.
06 · Chaos theory

The road to chaos

The logistic map and period doubling

An elementary-school equation: x ← r·x·(1−x). A very simple model of population. Change r and watch:

  • With r<3 the population settles on a single value.
  • Past r≈3, it oscillates between two values.
  • Then four. Then eight. Period doubling.
  • At r≈3.57 it explodes into chaos — and "windows" of order appear inside the chaos.

This is the Feigenbaum cascade, and astonishingly, the same ratios show up in very different systems. Chaos has universal numbers.

Try this Drag the vertical line. See a white window near r=3.83? Inside the chaos, order briefly returns. The universe quotes its own motifs.

07 · Emergence

Flock of boids

Three local rules, one global pattern

No single bird in a flock knows where the whole is heading. Each one follows three local rules (Reynolds, 1987):

① Separate from those too close

② Align with neighbors' heading

③ Move toward the group's center

From those three rules the flock emerges, appearing to have a mind of its own. No one is in charge. But it exists, it turns, it decides.

Try this Max out "separation" and drop "cohesion" to zero: the flock falls apart. Now do the opposite: it collapses into a single point. Balance between opposing forces is what makes the phenomenon appear.
08 · Fractals

Mandelbrot: the infinite within

The broken geometry of nature

Benoît Mandelbrot noticed that clouds aren't spheres, mountains aren't cones, coastlines can't be measured with a ruler. Nature has detail at every scale — it's fractal.

The set that bears his name is generated by a trivial formula: z ← z² + c. But as you zoom in, every region holds a copy-like version of the whole, and you discover motifs no one drew by hand. The part carries the whole — Morin's hologrammatic principle in pure geometry.
